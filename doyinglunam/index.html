What is Doying Lunam

The Mising Oral Tradition

Doying Lunam is the practice of storytelling at the heart of Mising cultural life — the art of passing down wisdom, cosmology, and memory through the spoken word.

The Mising people of the Brahmaputra plains have, for generations, preserved their history not in stone or paper but in the rhythm of the spoken word. Doying Lunam — the storytelling tradition — encompasses myths of creation, tales of river spirits, ancestor epics, moral fables, and explanations of the natural world.

Told by elders at dusk, woven into festivals like Dobur Uie and Po:Rag, and carried across the floodplains by the Mising diaspora, these stories form a living archive — one that faces the threat of silence in the age of rapid cultural change.

This project, led by @misingfolktales under the Cultural & Digital Creation Wing of TMSK, aims to bring these stories into the digital age — faithfully, beautifully, and with respect for the communities that carry them.

Ancestor Myth · Origin

Abotaro and Abotani

The First Ancestor of the Tani Peoples

In those ancient days, Abotani and Abotaro, sons of the creator Se-dí Me-lo, dwelled in the celestial harmony of Ki-líng Kangge. However, a bitter rivalry soon fractured their peace. Abotaro’s superior skills sparked envy in Abotani, leading to a fateful wager: whoever looked back at the other while working would become subordinate. Abotani failed the test, cementing his lower status. This divine discord eventually drove them from heaven to the Indian plains, establishing the ancestral lineage and distinct customs of the Mising people and their neighbors.

Origin of a Mibu

Religious leader of the people

In the ancient villages of Arunachal, the Mising people recount the mystical origin of the Mibu, their religious leader. It began when a young boy from the Mili clan vanished, only to reappear during a solemn rite held in his honor. He revealed that he had been spirited away by the Époms, forest spirits dwelling in a massive bo tree. Under their care, he was taught sacred a-bang chants and granted divine insight into the causes and remedies of diseases. This transformative journey bestowed upon him spiritual powers, leading the people to designate him as the first Mibu. To this day, it is believed that Époms continue to choose and mold young boys in the deep forests to carry on this sacred lineage of leadership and healing.

Spirits

Karpunpuli

Tale of the two sisters

In olden times, there lived two girls named Karpun and Puli, who were renowned throughout their village for their unmatched beauty and dancing skills. During the winter, they spent their days by the river designing intricate textile motifs, playfully undoing their work and blaming it on mice. They were also adept at catching fish from the sandy riverbeds and gathering sweet tarsin pursin herbs. As they grew into young women, their fame only increased. Their dancing to the tune of Sumbur sumbur nékova became a central highlight of the community, drawing massive crowds during the Ali a-ye Ligang festival, where everyone gathered to witness their grace and charm.

04
Matrimonial

A matrimonial tale

Mising folklore about clan matrimonial origins.

In the old times, a youth named So-bo wandered the lonely banks of the Di-ing until he spotted a maiden across the clear waters. Drawn by her song, he swam the river to meet her, and their informal union founded a lasting bond between the Doley and Pe-gu clans. To this day, their descendants honor this ancestral journey through their architecture; Doleys point their house beams westward, following So-bo’s upstream path, while Pe-gus point theirs eastward, marking the direction from which their clan first arrived.

The Dark Spot On The Moon

A Sisterly Rivalry Casts an Eternal Shadow on the Moon

In the dawn of time, sisters Do-nyi (Sun) and Po-lo (Moon) journeyed home to visit their mother. While Po-lo prepared thoughtful rice cakes, the envious Do-nyi, caught empty-handed, scavenged a foul decoy from the jungle. Upon their arrival, their mother was revolted by Do-nyi’s deceptive gift and hurled the vile packet in rage. The agile Sun ducked, leaving the innocent Moon to take the blow. The filth clung to Po-lo instantly, marking her silver face with the permanent dark spots we see in the night sky today.

Living Tradition

How Stories Are Told

Doying Lunam is not just what is told — it is how, when, and by whom. The tradition carries its own rituals, rhythms, and responsibilities.

🌙
Told After Dusk

Traditionally, Mising folktales are told in the evening — after the day's work is done and the community gathers. Telling certain stories in daylight is considered inauspicious.

🌾
Festival Storytelling

During Po:Rag, Dobur Uie, and other festivals, stories are recited as part of ceremony — sometimes by ritual specialists, sometimes by elders to assembled communities.

🎋
The Role of the Elder

The right to tell certain stories — especially sacred origin myths — belongs to elders and Dobur (ritual leaders). Their authority ensures stories are not distorted from generation to generation.
